# Design Direction

How to make the hero specific to the subject instead of a generic AI landing. Read before styling.

## Ground it in the subject

Before designing, name the concrete subject, its audience, and the page's one job. Pull palette, type, and the signature motion from the subject's own world — its materials and artifacts — not from a generic template. A deck of tarot-like cards suggests fanning motion, sacred geometry, gold-on-deep-color; a hardware device suggests exploded layers and precise machined motion. Let the product dictate.

## Avoid the AI-landing defaults

Current AI-generated designs cluster into three looks. Don't fall into them unless the brief explicitly asks:
1. Warm cream background + high-contrast serif + terracotta accent.
2. Near-black background + one acid-green/vermilion accent.
3. Broadsheet layout, hairline rules, zero radius, dense columns.

If a free design axis is heading toward one of these by default, spend it on something derived from the subject instead.

## Spend boldness in one place

Pick ONE signature element to be memorable (the scroll reveal itself usually is it) and keep everything around it quiet. Maximal motion + maximal everything-else = noise. Restraint reads as premium.

## Composition: separate the zones

The single most common failure is piling copy onto the focal visual. Decide a clear spatial split — e.g. headline + eyebrow + CTA in the top third, the animated product in the bottom two-thirds (mirrors how the product actually sits on a surface). Each zone reads independently. Verify with screenshots.

## Copy

Copy is design material, not decoration. For a hero:
- **Eyebrow**: what the thing is, plainly (category), not a slogan.
- **Headline**: the emotional hook, short, can be a two-part line where the second part is the turn (e.g. a topic + "and everything hidden").
- **Lede**: one or two sentences — who it's for + the concrete payload (a number helps: "88 cards", "6 layers").
- **CTA**: the literal action ("Order the deck"), same verb it produces downstream. No "Submit".

Avoid filler superlatives ("revolutionary", "innovative"). Be specific over clever.

## The 10-chapter scrollytelling structure (only for full long-scroll pages)

If the user wants a *whole* scroll-story page (not just a hero), a proven beat structure is: HERO (curiosity) → the why/pain → the break (contrast) → the solution → how it works → proof → who it's for → what's inside → guarantee/case → price + CTA. Chapter 01 and the final chapter should rhyme visually (open loop / close loop). Each chapter gets one visual that continues the thought rather than illustrating it.

This is optional and orthogonal to the hero technique — most jobs only need the single hero section. Don't impose 10 chapters on a request that just wants an animated hero.
